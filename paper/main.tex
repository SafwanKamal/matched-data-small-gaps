\title{Matched data, small gaps: a pre-registered comparison of fine-tuned classifiers, retrieval-prompted language models and a typed-decision model for intent and out-of-scope decisions}
\author{Safwan Kamal}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
We compare a fine-tuned 150M classifier and retrieval-prompted 27--31B language models on intent and out-of-scope decisions, each given the same labelled data, in protocols frozen before any request; a hosted typed-decision model is a secondary system. On Banking77 the classifier is 1.4 points behind the primary general model, not significant after correction, and equivalence within 2.5 points is not shown. The one significant pre-registered difference (CLINC150, $-1.9$ points, Holm $p=.007$) splits about equally between scope and intent, and its in-scope part holds on items we had never inspected (post hoc). MASSIVE cannot be ranked: its result depends on which domains are held out. A post hoc combination does not replicate, and two frontier models run with reasoning off were not significantly more accurate on 100 sessions per set, at 17 to 44 times the uncached API cost. For prompted models, the out-of-scope decision moves with what the prompt shows. On BANKING77-OOS, retrieving in-scope examples lowered five models' rejection of unsupported same-domain requests by 19 to 30 points on fresh label-clean items, after we removed a label leak we found in that dataset; for the typed-decision model the drop is at its fixed threshold. Random examples move it much less (1 to 11 points). Labelled out-of-scope examples raise rejection for the requests that retrieve them. No mitigation met its pre-registered criterion for most models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Typed-decision models such as Jev \citep{deusser2026jev} prompted this study. Jev returns a probability for each of a fixed set of typed options, which places it between a fine-tuned classifier and a prompted language model. That raised a broader question: given the same labelled data, which kind of system should decide whether an assistant handles a message and what it asks for? Our pre-registered comparison is between classifiers and prompted language models; Jev, added after those protocols were frozen, is a secondary system.

Before an assistant acts on a message it decides whether the message is something it handles (\emph{scope}) and which operation it asks for (\emph{intent}). The usual choices are a small classifier fine-tuned on labelled examples, a prompted general language model, or a cascade from the first to the second. Recent work finds that a cascade can beat both \citep{chen2025fcslm} or approach the general model at lower cost \citep{zaera2025udril,arora2024intent}, because the classifier names intents better and the general model recognises out-of-scope requests better \citep{rodrigues2026nlu}. Those comparisons usually equip one side better than the other. The classifier thresholds its softmax, which is weak on same-domain out-of-scope requests \citep{zhang2022oos,podolskiy2021mahalanobis}, or the general model answers without the labelled examples the classifier was trained on, which retrieval would provide \citep{milios2023manylabels,loukas2023penny}.

Our own exploratory comparison equipped both sides and found the retrieval-prompted model ahead on every semantic intent set. An internal review attributed that lead to three artefacts:
a threshold objective that over-rejected in-scope requests;
borrowed out-of-scope sets that capitalisation separates;
and labelled out-of-scope rows in the model's retrieval pool that the classifier never saw.
We therefore froze a confirmatory protocol before any request, with matched data, normalised text, native or held-out-domain out-of-scope data, four pre-registered systems, a clustered test with Holm correction, and the sentence to print for each outcome (\cref{sec:confirmatory}). A second frozen protocol tested an observation from the exploratory data: that retrieving in-scope examples lowers a model's rejection of unsupported same-domain requests (\cref{sec:operating}). Jev was added under its own protocol, frozen after the first two had results, and as an arm of two further frozen protocols, which tested the causes of the effect and the robustness of the MASSIVE result.

Our contributions are three. First, a matched pre-registered comparison: on Banking77 the classifier and the general model are not significantly different, and equivalence is not shown; the one significant gap, 1.9 points on CLINC150, splits about equally between scope and intent; MASSIVE cannot be ranked; and a cascade comes within 0.4 points of the model alone at 40\% of its API cost, plus hosting the classifier. Second, evidence on what sets a prompted model's out-of-scope decision. Retrieved in-scope examples lower the rejection of unsupported same-domain requests on BANKING77-OOS for four general models and, at its fixed threshold, a typed-decision model, replicated on fresh leak-free items. Random examples move it much less. With ten options, CLINC single-domain shows no drop. Labelled out-of-scope examples raise rejection for the requests that retrieve them. Third, a record of what went wrong, as a checklist for such comparisons. The record covers an exploratory headline that did not survive the confirmatory design, a label leak in a public benchmark, and an item-reuse error in our own freshness rule.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Classifiers, general models and cascades.} Zero- and few-shot general models trail fine-tuned classifiers on fine-grained intent sets but detect out-of-scope requests better \citep{rodrigues2026nlu,arora2024intent,castillo2025oos}. Cascades that route a classifier's uncertain cases to a general model beat both on Banking77, CLINC150 and HWU64 \citep{chen2025fcslm} and approach the general model's accuracy at lower cost \citep{zaera2025udril,arora2024intent}; routing between general models is studied by \citet{bouchard2026escalation} and \citet{llmrouterbench2026}.

\paragraph{Retrieval and few-shot learning.} Retrieving demonstrations similar to the input helps in-context learning \citep{liu2022kate} and closes much of the intent gap to fine-tuning \citep{milios2023manylabels,loukas2023penny}, and many-shot in-context learning keeps improving with more examples \citep{bertsch2024longicl}. Fine-tuning breaks even with prompting at about 100 labelled samples \citep{pecher2025breakeven}; SetFit is a strong few-shot fine-tuning baseline \citep{tunstall2022setfit}. Demonstrations shift a model's label prior \citep{zhao2021calibrate}, and what models learn from demonstrations is partly the label space and input distribution rather than the input--label mapping \citep{min2022demonstrations}. The effect we report is narrower than a global prior shift: random in-domain examples move the scope decision much less than retrieved ones, and the drop is larger where the retrieved labels concentrate on one label. Contextual calibration \citep{zhao2021calibrate} corrects a global prior and is untested here. \citet{arora2024intent} report that out-of-scope detection by prompted models degrades with more labels, which bears on our option-count test.

\paragraph{Out-of-scope detection.} Classifier scores include the maximum softmax probability \citep{hendrycks2017baseline}, energy \citep{liu2020energy}, Mahalanobis distance \citep{lee2018mahalanobis,podolskiy2021mahalanobis} and nearest-neighbour distance \citep{sun2022knn}; trained boundaries include \citet{zhang2021adb}. \citet{zhang2022oos} separate out-of-domain from same-domain out-of-scope (BANKING77-OOS, CLINC single-domain) and show that the second defeats confidence-based detectors. \citet{li2024catchall} exclude HWU64's catch-all intents when building an out-of-scope benchmark, and \citet{yilmaz2022d2u} attribute weaker out-of-scope detection on HWU64 to its generic utterances. Training with labelled outliers improves detection \citep{hendrycks2019oe}.

\paragraph{Typed-decision models.} \citet{deusser2026jev} benchmark Jev on 37 data sets, and \citet{tang2026audit} audit the early evidence on typed-decision models. \citet{rao2026jev} find that Jev and language-model rubric judges err on the same items, so a Jev-first cascade gains little. \citet{anyjev2026} and \citet{li2026llmasjev} read Jev-style option probabilities out of general language models. We add a matched comparison on intent and scope in which Jev is a secondary system.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\input{fig_design}

\Cref{fig:design} shows how the experiments connect.

\paragraph{Decisions and endpoint.} Each turn needs a scope decision and, if in scope, an intent decision; a turn is right when both are. The primary endpoint is weighted turn accuracy, $0.8\times$ the share of in-scope turns kept with the right intent plus $0.2\times$ the share of out-of-scope turns rejected, which matches a 20\% out-of-scope prior and does not depend on the rate in the sessions. Turns form five-turn sessions; session success is secondary.

\paragraph{Data.} \Cref{tab:data} lists the confirmatory sets, built from Banking77 \citep{casanueva2020banking77}, MASSIVE \citep{fitzgerald2022massive} and CLINC150 \citep{larson2019clinc}. CLINC150 uses its native out-of-scope test queries, and Banking77 borrows the same CLINC150 queries. MASSIVE uses utterances from three whole domains held out of its label set (email, iot, play), drawn with a fixed seed from the 16 domains other than \emph{general} and \emph{qa}, which we exclude as catch-alls, following \citet{li2024catchall}. Those are sibling domains of retained skills such as music and social. All text is lower-cased, and punctuation other than apostrophes is removed, for every system and pool. The sets were meant to contain only test items no earlier session used; that held for MASSIVE but not for Banking77 and CLINC150 (\cref{sec:freshness}).

\paragraph{Systems.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Specialist:} an Ettin 150M encoder \citep{weller2026ettin} with a mean-pooled classification head, retrained on the normalised data. It uses three seeds and a learning rate chosen on development data (for MASSIVE, on the earlier 60-label task, not re-swept). Scope comes from a development-selected detector, with a threshold weighted by the 20\% prior. The development out-of-scope items are 100 CLINC150 out-of-scope validation queries for Banking77 and CLINC150, and 100 development items from the held-out domains for MASSIVE.
  \item \textbf{General models:} Gemma 4 31B (primary) and Qwen3.5 27B (secondary). Each answers scope and intent at temperature 0 with reasoning off, after 20 BM25-nearest \citep{robertson2009bm25} in-scope training examples. The pool contains no out-of-scope rows, and labels are shown by name only, without descriptions.
  \item \textbf{Jev 1.13.0} \citep{deusser2026jev}: a hosted ``typed-decision'' model from TypeSafe, pinned to one version, that answers a yes/no question as a probability and a choice question as a distribution over the given labels in one call. Its weights and architecture are not public, and its API bills 113 to 1,259 output tokens per call in our runs. It receives the same 20 neighbours, labelled with intent names, and its scope decision uses a fixed 0.5 threshold; its interface therefore differs from the general models' prompt, and comparisons with them are secondary.
  \item \textbf{Combination} (pre-registered): Gemma's scope, with the specialist's intent when its probability is at least 0.9.
  \item \textbf{Cascade} (pre-registered): the 40\% of turns with the lowest specialist confidence go to Gemma.
\end{itemize}
Hosts, precision, prompts and hyperparameters are in \cref{app:systems}.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Paired differences are tested by sign flips of cluster sums. Clusters are gold intents; each out-of-scope turn is its own cluster. Intervals are 95\% cluster bootstrap. The primary family is specialist against Gemma and combination against Gemma on each set, with Holm correction \citep{holm1979}. Banking77 was to be tested for equivalence at $\pm2.5$ points. Everything else is secondary and unadjusted, or post hoc where labelled.

On Banking77 the 1.4-point gap is not significant after correction. On MASSIVE the specialist is 4.4 points ahead, but the test cannot detect differences below about 9 points there (clustering its out-of-scope turns by source label, $p=.55$), and Qwen is 2.6 points ahead of the specialist here and 1.9 to 3.4 points ahead on all three further draws (\cref{app:massive}), so the ranking depends on the model. The Jev protocol's gate, Jev's scope with the specialist's intent, is 1.1 points ahead of the specialist on Banking77 ($p=.0005$) and not significantly different on CLINC150 ($+0.9$, $p=.12$) or MASSIVE ($-0.7$, $p=.95$; secondary, unadjusted). These gaps are not artefacts of example order: two further orderings of Gemma's 20 retrieved examples changed its weighted accuracy by at most 0.16 points on CLINC150 and 0.41 on MASSIVE, and left the CLINC150 gap between $-1.75$ and $-1.90$ (\cref{app:gh}).

\paragraph{Where the gaps come from.} Splitting each gap into a scope part and an intent part, the CLINC150 gap divides evenly (0.9 scope, 1.0 intent). Our pre-registered headline rule therefore forbids saying that the difference lies in the out-of-scope decision. MASSIVE's 4.4 points are all scope. Gemma keeps 97\% of in-scope turns but rejects only 23\% of the held-out-domain requests: 5\% of \emph{play} requests, which it maps onto the retained music skills, and 35\% of \emph{email} requests. The specialist rejects 78\% and 81\% of these, with a threshold set, as pre-registered, on development items from the same held-out domains; set from in-scope development data alone, it rejects 24 to 54\% and scores 73.9 to 79.3 (post hoc). The frontier models also reject under 10\% of \emph{play} requests (\cref{app:frontier}). On three further seeded draws of held-out domains (a sensitivity analysis that reuses MASSIVE test text; \cref{app:massive}), specialist minus Gemma is $-1.0$, $-1.7$ and $+0.3$ points, none significant, and Gemma rejects 61 to 68\% of held-out-domain requests. Two of the three further draws also held out music, the sibling of play, so they removed play's sibling as well; whether the sibling explains the confirmatory result is untested. Further post hoc checks are in \cref{app:massive}.

\paragraph{Freshness (post hoc).}
\label{sec:freshness}
After the run, an audit found that 832 of Banking77's 1,295 in-scope turns had been used before. So had 65 of its 184 out-of-scope turns and 78 of CLINC150's 185. They appeared in our exploratory BANKING77-OOS sessions. That set is a lower-cased copy of the Banking77 test split and reuses CLINC out-of-scope queries, and our freshness rule had matched exact text. The models never saw these items before, but we had. On never-seen turns no primary comparison is significant (\cref{tab:primary}); CLINC150's gap halves to $-0.94$ [$-2.14$, $+0.35$]. Split by component, the in-scope part of the CLINC150 gap is unchanged on never-seen turns ($-2.19$ [$-3.12$, $-1.32$], $p=.0001$). The change comes from the out-of-scope turns: specialist $-$ Gemma is $+4.0$ on the 107 never-seen ones and $-7.3$ on the 78 seen before. Neither out-of-scope subset differs significantly ($p=.17$ and $p=.09$, post hoc); since the models never saw these items, the change reflects which out-of-scope items are compared.

\paragraph{Frontier models and cost.} Kimi K3 and Claude Opus 5 are never significantly different from Gemma (largest: Opus on CLINC150, $+1.8$, $p=.065$), though 100 sessions per set cannot exclude gains of 2 to 3 points. Uncached at list prices they cost 17 to 21 and 36 to 44 times as much per session. The specialist answers in 11\,ms at batch size 1 on one consumer GPU, against median calls of 1.0 to 2.8\,s, and hosting it costs less than calling Gemma on every turn above about 47,000 to 106,000 sessions a month for an assumed \$50-a-month host (\cref{app:frontier,app:systems}).

On our exploratory BANKING77-OOS sessions, Gemma rejected 70\% of requests for held-out Banking77 intents zero-shot and 38\% with retrieved in-scope examples. A second frozen protocol (run D) tested this on items not used in the exploratory sessions (462 BANKING77-OOS and 332 CLINC single-domain items had been confirmatory turns; \cref{app:record}), with four general models from four families and two same-domain splits: BANKING77-OOS \citep{zhang2022oos}, and CLINC single-domain out-of-scope for banking and for credit cards. There were four arms: zero-shot, retrieval, retrieval with a scope instruction, and retrieval with four fixed off-topic out-of-scope examples. The Jev protocol repeated the first three arms.

\paragraph{Retrieval moves models towards acceptance on one split.} On BANKING77-OOS, retrieval lowered rejection of held-out requests for all four general models, by 24 to 40 points (Holm $p\le.0008$ each), and for Jev by 45 points (Holm $p=.0002$; \cref{tab:suppression}). It also raised in-scope acceptance from 81--92\% to 95--99\%. On CLINC single-domain, with each domain's own 10 options, it lowered rejection for no model; with 20 options it did for three (below).

An audit then found that the published BANKING77-OOS training split mislabels 366 of 5,905 rows. 145 of them are utterances of 17 held-out intents filed under in-scope labels, so the retrieval pool leaked held-out intents, and the models copied the leaked labels. On the 10 held-out intents that never leak, the drop is 14 to 38 points for all five models ($p\le.009$). It survives the leak but is smaller.

\paragraph{Mechanism (post hoc for the general models, pre-registered for Jev).} Accepted held-out requests go to nearby in-scope labels (D2 items below; \cref{app:posthoc}). For the four general models, the chosen label is among the 20 retrieved labels for 92 to 99\% of accepted held-out requests; zero-shot, 83 to 94\% of accepted requests already go to those labels, so retrieval mainly raises how many requests are accepted rather than where they go. Gemma, for example, maps all 33 identity-verification requests it accepts to \emph{why verify identity}. The drop is larger where the retrieved labels concentrate on one label (for Qwen, 29 points against 8). In signal-detection terms \citep{macmillan2005detection}, retrieval moves the general models' criterion towards acceptance by 0.7 to 1.1 standard deviations on leak-free items (\cref{app:d2}).

Jev returns probabilities, so its discrimination was measured directly. Its pre-registered readout on the as-run items was an operating-point shift: AUROC unchanged ($+0.003$ [$-0.03$, $+0.04$]), and the mean in-scope probability on held-out requests up by 0.29. On leak-free items its AUROC rises slightly ($+0.03$ [$+0.007$, $+0.068$]) while that mean probability still rises by 0.17. At equal in-scope acceptance its rejection does not fall: on the fresh D2 items, with 95\% of in-scope requests kept, retrieval raises it from 61\% to 82\% (post hoc). Jev's drop in \cref{tab:suppression,tab:d2} is therefore at its fixed 0.5 threshold, which retrieval moves most in-scope and many held-out requests past. For the general models, which answer yes or no, no such comparison is possible; in aggregate their change looks like a shift towards acceptance with level or slightly better discrimination.

On CLINC single-domain the shift is smaller (the interval excludes zero for two of four general models), and discrimination improves (Jev: AUROC $+0.11$). Retrieval closeness does not explain the difference between the splits once the leak is removed (AUROC of top-1 similarity 0.90 against 0.91).

\paragraph{Replication and causes (pre-registered, run D2).} A third frozen protocol (run D2) used validation items. On BANKING77-OOS they were fresh and label-clean: 500 in-scope and 388 held-out items, from 23 intents, with all 366 mislabelled rows removed from the pool. On CLINC single-domain there were 1,000 in-scope items, which had served as run D's specialist development data (never sent to a model; \cref{app:record}), and 575 held-out items. The protocol added three arms (\cref{tab:d2,fig:operating}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The drop replicates} for all five models, by 19 to 30 points (Holm $p\le.0006$).
  \item \textbf{Relevance drives it.} Twenty random in-scope examples leave rejection 13 to 21 points higher than the 20 retrieved ones (Holm $p\le.004$ for all five), within 1 to 11 points of zero-shot.
  \item \textbf{Repeating the rule partly helps.} Repeating the scope rule after the examples, immediately before the request, recovers 7 to 19 points of rejection for Qwen, Llama, DeepSeek and Jev (Holm $p\le.008$); Gemma does not move ($+1.5$, $p=.13$). By run D's ``works'' criterion this is a mitigation for Llama only (79\% recovered at $-0.6$ in-scope points). The arm repeats the rule rather than moving it.
  \item \textbf{Option count is not established.} Pooling CLINC single-domain's two domains into one 20-option prompt lowers rejection by 14 to 25 points for Gemma, Llama and DeepSeek, but the pooled prompt changes more than the option count (\cref{app:d2}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Labelled unsupported examples move it the other way.} In a paired secondary arm of the confirmatory run, we added 250 labelled out-of-scope rows to Gemma's pool. These rows came from the same three held-out domains as the test requests. Gemma's rejection of those requests rose from 23\% to 91\%, and its weighted accuracy rose by 13.3 points [12.1, 14.6]. On the three further MASSIVE draws, rows from the tested domains again raised accuracy, for Gemma by 5.1 to 6.5 points and for Qwen by 2.3 to 4.0 (Holm $p\le.0003$ for all six). Rows from three \emph{other} unsupported domains raised weighted accuracy significantly in 1 of 6 tests (Gemma, draw 1, $+2.0$), but raised rejection in all 6 cells (post hoc, $+2.4$ to $+8.8$ points). That gain falls on the requests for which such a row is retrieved ($+5$ to $+16$ points, against about zero for the others). Labelled out-of-scope examples help a prompted model on the requests that retrieve them (\cref{fig:rows}), as outlier exposure helps classifiers \citep{hendrycks2019oe}.

\paragraph{Mitigations.} Neither pre-registered mitigation met its criterion, which was to recover half of the lost rejection at a cost of at most 2 points of in-scope accuracy. The instruction recovered 10 to 42\% on BANKING77-OOS and none for Jev. On CLINC single-domain it raised rejection by 15 to 27 points for the general models, and by 5 points for Jev. On either split it cost at most 1.8 in-scope points. Off-topic out-of-scope examples did not help. In D2, repeating the scope rule after the examples met the criterion for Llama only. Two post hoc rules on the saved answers also fail it (\cref{app:posthoc}).

\section{Discussion}

On our opening question, the pre-registered tests do not separate classifiers from prompted models on overall accuracy, but neither do they show the two to be equivalent: there is no significant difference on Banking77, where equivalence within 2.5 points is not shown; one significant gap of 1.9 points on CLINC150; and no ranking on MASSIVE. Differences between individual models can exceed the difference between kinds. On MASSIVE, Gemma and Qwen, given the same prompts and examples, rejected 23\% and 68\% of held-out-domain requests (descriptive). For a team choosing a system, the kind of system therefore says less than a check of the out-of-scope decision for the specific model and data. Four observations follow, each tagged with its evidence:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Matched and equipped, the classifier and the general model are not separated by our tests.} On Banking77 the 1.4-point gap is not significant and equivalence is not shown; on CLINC150 the general model is 1.9 points ahead; MASSIVE's direction depends on the model (pre-registered). Jev given the same examples is level with Gemma on Banking77 and CLINC150, and two frontier models were not significantly more accurate on 100 sessions per set, though gains of 2 to 3 points are not excluded (secondary). Claims that one approach dominates should be checked against an equipped version of the other.
  \item \textbf{For a prompted model, the out-of-scope decision moves with what the prompt shows.} Retrieved in-scope examples lower the rejection of unsupported same-domain requests on BANKING77-OOS (pre-registered twice, the second time on fresh leak-free items for five models). Relevant examples cause most of it. For the typed-decision model the drop is at its fixed threshold: at equal in-scope acceptance, retrieval raised its rejection (post hoc). They did not lower rejection on CLINC single-domain with 10 options, and we could not establish why. Labelled out-of-scope examples raise rejection for the requests that retrieve them (MASSIVE, four draws). Repeating the scope rule after the examples helped four of five models but met our mitigation criterion for one. The classifier's threshold, by contrast, is tuned on development out-of-scope data, which on MASSIVE came from the tested domains. A fair comparison reports each system's operating point, not only its accuracy.
  \item \textbf{Comparisons of this kind are fragile.} Our exploratory MASSIVE result (Gemma $+5.3$) did not survive the confirmatory design, which changed the label set, the out-of-scope source, the casing and the pool at once. A public benchmark leaked held-out intents into its training split. Our own freshness rule missed lower-cased copies. We suggest reporting, for any classifier-versus-model scope comparison: how out-of-scope data was sourced and whether surface cues separate it, what data each system's scope decision could use, the objective behind every threshold, and a label audit of the benchmark.
  \item \textbf{The cascade's value is API cost} (pre-registered). It comes within 0.4 points of the general model at 40\% of its API cost, but it also needs the classifier hosted, which pays for itself only above about 79,000 to 177,000 sessions a month at \$50 a month.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{Data.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The confirmatory sets are assembled from single utterances of three English label sets.
  \item The supply of native out-of-scope data limits their size: the smallest detectable differences are 1.7, 1.9 and 8.8 points.
  \item Banking77 and CLINC150 items overlap our exploratory sessions, and run D's items overlap the confirmatory turns.
  \item The confirmatory MASSIVE draw held out sibling domains, and three further draws reuse its test text. Its in-scope turns cover half of its labels.
  \item In the confirmatory run, the labelled out-of-scope rows added to Gemma's pool come from the test domains.
  \item The two same-domain splits come from one benchmark family. One of them has a label leak, and they differ in option count.
  \item The base BANKING77-OOS prompt already contains a scope sentence.
  \item Labels are shown by name only; label descriptions might reduce sibling absorption.
  \item The option-count arm could reach significance for at most one model and changes more than the number of options.
  \item The further MASSIVE draws hold out six domains each, which removes sibling skills from the label set.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Models.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item General models ran once per prompt at temperature 0 with one retriever; other orders of the retrieved examples changed accuracy by at most 0.4 points.
  \item Retrieval was BM25 only, which needs no trained component. In an exploratory paired comparison on 400 earlier sessions per set, a dense retriever (all-mpnet-base-v2) changed Gemma's session success by $-2.2$ to $+2.5$ points, none significantly; its effect on the out-of-scope findings, such as how strongly the retrieved labels concentrate on one sibling, is untested.
  \item Moving one model between two fp8 hosts changed 9.5\% of its answers, which is the size of several reported differences.
  \item The two frontier models were chosen by the author among those accepting reasoning disabled; reasoning-on use was not tested.
  \item Jev's scope threshold was fixed at 0.5 and not tuned; a threshold set from in-scope data changes its out-of-scope results.
  \item Jev's per-call price is not reported by its API; all Jev use in the project, including exploratory calls before the frozen runs, cost \$4.27 (one \$5.00 credit with \$0.73 left; billing record in \texttt{artifacts/jev-cost/}), so a per-call price cannot be separated. Its interface (a fixed probability threshold, label names) differs from the general models' prompt.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Analyses.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The specialist uses development out-of-scope data, which on MASSIVE came from the held-out test domains; the prompted models use none.
  \item The signal-detection estimates for the general models are aggregate descriptions; under unequal variances the sign of the discrimination change is not determined for two of four models.
  \item CLINC150 and Banking77 are public and may be in the models' training data. A completion probe found 0.5\% verbatim completions for Gemma (Qwen refused the task), which does not rule contamination out.
\end{itemize}

\section{Protocols, amendments and withdrawn conclusions}
\label{app:record}

The confirmatory questions were chosen after more than twenty exploratory protocols on the same label sets. Six protocols govern this paper; each was committed before any of its requests, and each carries dated amendments. Their freeze times, their text as frozen and their item-list hashes are listed in \texttt{release/FREEZE\_TABLE.md}; the times are the author's git commit timestamps. OpenRouter's own request log, exported by the author, dates the first hosted request after each freeze 4 to 17 seconds after it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{confirmatory\_protocol.json}
  \item \texttt{retrieval\_suppression\_protocol.json}
  \item \texttt{jev\_rerun\_protocol.json}
  \item \texttt{retrieval\_mechanism\_protocol.json}
  \item \texttt{massive\_robustness\_protocol.json}
  \item \texttt{gh\_protocol.json}
\end{itemize}
Within the confirmatory protocol, run A is Gemma, run B Qwen, run C Gemma with labelled out-of-scope rows in its pool, and run I the frontier models; run D is the retrieval-suppression protocol and run D2 the mechanism protocol. The amendments are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the frontier model was changed after Qwen3.8 2.4T refused requests with reasoning disabled;
  \item a plumbing-check cap was raised;
  \item DeepSeek V4 Pro was moved between hosts after rate limits;
  \item errata record the freshness problem (\cref{sec:freshness}) and the BANKING77-OOS label leak;
  \item run D's BANKING77-OOS items were fresh with respect to the BANKING77-OOS sessions only: 462 of its 1,077 items had appeared as confirmatory Banking77 turns, and 332 of its 1,700 CLINC single-domain items as confirmatory CLINC150 turns;
  \item before freezing D2, its CLINC single-domain in-scope items were allowed although they had served as run D's specialist development data (never sent to a model);
  \item D2 was switched to one process per model about an hour after its requests began (a request-loop change only; its note miscounts the finished cells), and the per-process caps together exceeded the run cap, which spend never approached;
  \item the cost figure stage of \texttt{confirmatory.py} was edited after the run (presentation only).
\end{itemize}
Two parallel processes briefly sent 76 duplicate requests, and spend was reconciled from saved rows. Every request of every experiment was answered; parse failures were zero in all runs.

Conclusions of our earlier drafts that these runs withdrew or narrowed:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item that the retrieval-prompted model overtakes the classifier on Banking77, MASSIVE and CLINC150;
  \item that the combination of the model's scope and the classifier's intent is significantly better on Banking77;
  \item that the difference lies in the out-of-scope decision;
  \item that BANKING77-OOS held-out requests resemble the retrieval pool more closely than CLINC single-domain ones (an artefact of the leak);
  \item that Jev is a weak scope gate. That came from zero-shot runs on our exploratory sessions; with retrieval it is level with Gemma on two sets;
  \item that the systems differ mainly in where they put the out-of-scope threshold (draft 3 abstract; contradicted by the pre-registered headline rule);
  \item that restating the scope rule after the examples is a cheap general lever (it met the mitigation criterion for one of five models);
  \item that labelled out-of-scope rows help only when they come from the tested domains (rows from other domains raise rejection for the requests that retrieve them);
  \item that Gemma's host was slow throughout runs G and H (the saved latencies give medians of about one second).
\end{enumerate}
Studies cut from this paper remain in the release record: typed state against stated decisions, model-to-model handoff, encoder against decoder, a local orchestrator, de-escalation and cascades between two general models.

\section{Post hoc analyses}
\label{app:posthoc}

\paragraph{Mitigation rules.} Accepting only when the specialist also accepts recovers 42 to 96\% of the loss at 2.0 to 3.0 in-scope points; a similarity floor costs 11 to 16 points; both fail the criterion on held-out intents that the development items did not cover. The full tables are in \texttt{docs/posthoc-round3.md} and \texttt{docs/posthoc-round3b.md}. The development out-of-scope items covered 5 of the 27 held-out test intents on BANKING77-OOS, and 2 of 5 per CLINC single-domain domain. The specialist rejects 64.8\% of covered-intent requests against 44.5\% of uncovered ones. On uncovered intents only:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Classifier veto} (accept only if the specialist also accepts): recovers 42 to 96\% of the lost rejection on BANKING77-OOS, at 2.0 to 3.0 points of in-scope accuracy, with every interval extending below $-2$.
  \item \textbf{Similarity floor:} costs 11 to 16 points.
  \item \textbf{Neighbour-label rule:} changes little.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sibling labels (D2, BANKING77-OOS, retrieval).} Gemma's accepted held-out requests go mostly to sibling labels: verify my identity and unable to verify identity to \emph{why verify identity} (17 and 16), pin blocked to \emph{change pin} (15), top-up by bank transfer charge to \emph{transfer fee charged} (10). The chosen label is among the 20 retrieved labels for 92 to 99\% of accepted held-out requests and is the top-1 neighbour's label for 43 to 58\% (four general models). Split at the median concentration of the retrieved labels, rejection falls by 30 against 18 points (Gemma), 29 against 8 (Qwen), 25 against 23 (Llama) and 30 against 22 (DeepSeek).

\paragraph{Label audit.} BANKING77-OOS was matched to the original Banking77 labels after lower-casing:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 366 mislabelled training rows, of which 145 are leaked held-out intents;
  \item 32 in-scope test items with wrong gold intents;
  \item 36 ``held-out'' test items that are in-scope intents by their original labels.
\end{itemize}
Our files are byte-identical to the upstream release.

\section{Run D2 details}
\label{app:d2}

\paragraph{Signal detection.} As an aggregate description we treat scope as a detection task \citep{macmillan2005detection}, separating discrimination ($d'$) from the criterion ($c$, readiness to accept). On leak-free BANKING77-OOS items, retrieval moves the four general models' criterion towards acceptance by 0.7 to 1.1 standard deviations. Allowing unequal variances (zROC slopes 0.6 to 1.25), discrimination improves for Llama and DeepSeek at every slope, while for Gemma and Qwen the sign of the change depends on the slope.

\paragraph{Option count.} With CLINC single-domain's two domains pooled into one 20-option prompt, retrieval lowers rejection by 14 to 25 points for Gemma (43 to 29\%), Llama (45 to 20\%) and DeepSeek (34 to 16\%), by 5 points for Jev, and not for Qwen. The difference from the 10-option prompts is Holm-significant only for Llama ($-29$ points, $p=.04$), but with eight held-out-intent clusters and Holm over five models, $.04$ is the smallest attainable adjusted $p$. The pooled prompt also adds sibling labels from the other domain, mixes the retrieval pools and changes the desk description, and its effect differs in sign between the two domains, so this arm does not isolate option count.

\section{Example order and contamination (runs G and H)}
\label{app:gh}

A frozen protocol (\texttt{gh\_protocol.json}) re-ran Gemma on the confirmatory CLINC150 and MASSIVE turns with the 20 retrieved examples most similar first and in a random order, in addition to the original least-similar-first order. Weighted accuracy ranged over 0.16 points on CLINC150 and 0.41 on MASSIVE; 0.6 to 4.5\% of answers changed. A contamination probe asked Gemma and Qwen to complete the second half of 200 Banking77 and 200 CLINC150 test utterances. Gemma completed 0.5\% verbatim. Its completions resembled their own endings more than other endings of the same intent (token F1 0.18 against 0.08 on Banking77); this probe cannot tell that apart from memorisation. Qwen refused 350 of 400 requests, so its rows say little. The pre-registered criterion for memorisation (a significant own-ending advantage and more than 5\% verbatim) was not met.

\section{Exploratory record}
\label{app:exploratory}

On our earlier sessions, a model retrieving from exactly the rows the specialist was trained on was never significantly behind it at any training-set size. This used one seed, one model, and a pool with out-of-scope rows. At one example per intent no specialist was usable, while nearest-neighbour and centroid classifiers on the same rows got 14\% of Banking77 sessions against about 49\% for the retrieval-prompted model.

On MultiDoGO support chats, the specialist was more accurate on the dataset's ``out of domain'' label and, by under one point, on its dialogue-act labels. Gemma was more accurate on task intents; Qwen 27B's difference there was not significant.
